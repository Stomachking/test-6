2026 Asia Mid-Year Outlook

From AI boom to energy resilience: the next phase of Asia's growth story

Yuxuan Tang, Cameron Chui and John Li · J.P. Morgan Private Bank · Published 6 Aug 2026 · Condensed reading edition of the original article. Select any passage to highlight it or add a note.

Executive summary

  • Asia is a prime beneficiary of the global AI build-out, and the region's export surge should keep powering growth. How durable that is depends on where each country sits in the value chain and how broad its industrial base is.
  • The Iran conflict has been a stress test for a region full of large energy importers. Outcomes diverged widely according to pricing power, fiscal room and the health of external balances.
  • China's economy is increasingly K-shaped: exports carry growth while domestic demand lags behind an oil shock and a still-soft housing market. Reflation will be gradual because policy stays targeted rather than broad.
  • Japan is balancing fiscal sustainability against macro stability. Subsidies have contained inflation, but a weak yen and strains in the bond market are pushing policymakers toward action.
  • Investment opportunities remain across asset classes: equities in Korea, Taiwan, China and select Japanese sectors; Asian high-yield USD bonds; and selected currencies.

Where Asia standsA tale of two Asias

The outlook published late last year flagged two things: Asia's export resilience in the face of tariff uncertainty, and its unique seat in the global AI supply chain. The first half of 2026 confirmed both, but with a twist. An AI-driven capex cycle lifted growth across much of the region, while escalation in the Middle East exposed the flip side: Asia hosts many of the world's largest net energy importers.

The backdrop now looks more constructive. Doubts about the longevity of the AI cycle are surfacing, yet the export tailwind should persist for economies with strategic positions in semiconductors and technology, and with oil apparently past its peak the energy drag should fade. The picture is far from uniform, though. Capital outflows, fiscal pressure, balance-of-payments strain and currency instability are shaping individual markets, which makes Asia increasingly a story of divergence.

ExportsAsia the exporter: can the AI dividend last?

Export exuberance is the dominant growth driver. AI-related capex has lifted demand for chips, memory, servers, networking gear and related electronics, offsetting tariff noise and muted consumer demand elsewhere. The gains are uneven: economies deep in the AI supply chain have benefited most, while those with limited AI exposure saw only a modest uplift.

Taiwan is heading for a second straight year of double-digit export growth on the back of advanced chip fabrication. Korea's export growth jumped from 8% last year to 54% in the first half, driven by tight supply in high-end memory. Japan gained through semiconductor equipment, precision machinery and automation. China remains central as a manufacturing and assembly hub for mature-node chips, optical components and power electronics, and Southeast Asia, led by Malaysia, Singapore and Vietnam, is attracting electronics manufacturing and data-centre investment. India, Indonesia and the Philippines, less integrated into the hardware chain, captured fewer gains.

The authors believe the AI dividend is durable despite market volatility, for two reasons. First, position in the value chain matters: visibility on hyperscaler and cloud investment over the next one to two years is strong, so firm memory prices and compute demand should keep supporting Taiwan and Korea, while economies further from the frontier rely more on volume, assembly margins and incentives. Second, breadth of industrial diversification matters: concentrated economies are exposed to sector shocks, whereas broad manufacturing bases such as China and Japan are more resilient.

The deeper risk is that the AI boom reinforces K-shaped growth. The cycle is capital-intensive and its benefits cluster in firms, sectors and regions closest to the technological frontier, so strong exports and earnings may not translate into broad income gains or stronger domestic demand.

EnergyAsia the importer: faring through the oil shock

With roughly 90% of oil passing through the Strait of Hormuz bound for Asia, the Middle East conflict exposed long-standing vulnerabilities rooted in scarce domestic energy and energy-intensive growth models. Higher prices hurt input costs, terms of trade, current accounts and, where subsidies expanded, fiscal balances.

Resilience varied. Korea and Taiwan leaned on strong industrial pricing power, with margins holding or even expanding on AI demand. Japan passed a sizeable supplementary budget to subsidise energy and limit inflation pass-through. India moved quickly to secure supply, diversify sourcing, cut import duties and expand subsidies. China was mixed: oil imports fell sharply as reserves were drawn down, yet parts of industry stayed weak as fuel price controls squeezed refiners and safety inspections disrupted coal output. In Southeast Asia, Singapore and Malaysia coped well thanks to fiscal buffers and low import dependence respectively, while Thailand and the Philippines slowed more and saw external balances deteriorate.

Looking ahead, country-specific factors will dominate. Japan and India could face growing fiscal strain if the conflict drags on. China's pressures should ease as energy prices settle, helped by a diversified energy mix. Across parts of South and Southeast Asia, however, balance-of-payments pressure may persist even after oil stabilises, pointing to deeper structural weaknesses.

ChinaA widening K-shaped economy

The constructive start-of-year view on China, built on export strength and early signs of domestic recovery, played out on the export side and then some, but the growth engine is lopsided: external demand is doing the heavy lifting while domestic activity struggles to keep pace.

Exports acted as the economy's locomotive in the first half. Post-Covid, China's export success has been volume-led, with the "new three" industries of solar, batteries and electric vehicles as the showcase; auto exports climbed from around one million units five years ago to nearly eight million, making China the world's largest car exporter. This year leadership broadened into AI-linked goods: machinery and electronics exports rose 20.1% year on year and made up 63.5% of total exports, with both volumes and prices rising in several categories. Memory chips illustrate the shift: Korea leads in cutting-edge products such as HBM, but China has matured in mainstream DRAM, and related exports grew roughly 86% in the first half, a meaningful part of it from higher prices rather than quantity alone.

Domestic demand, by contrast, is stuck in first gear. Five years into the property downturn, household confidence remains fragile; Tier-1 housing shows green shoots but national gauges are still falling. Retail sales grew just 1.3% in January to June, down from about 5% a year earlier. A new Five-Year Plan for Expanding Consumption targets RMB 60tn of retail sales by 2030, implying a cautious 3.7% annual pace; it is not a stimulus package, so the recovery is expected to be gradual and uneven.

Reflation remains an unfinished puzzle. The energy shock briefly pushed producer prices positive, but that was a supply effect rather than a fix for the underlying imbalance, and weak consumption limits the ability of mid- and downstream firms to pass on costs. For the second half and 2027, exports should remain the main driver, easing energy costs should support industry and profits, and policy support is expected to arrive through faster deployment of existing fiscal resources and fine-tuning PBoC easing rather than a large stimulus.

Japan(Still) walking a tightrope

Japan is balancing reflation, fiscal credibility and currency stability. The first-half fiscal agenda was shaped by the need to shield households from energy costs: gasoline subsidies were topped up from contingency reserves in March, and in June the government finalised a JPY 3.1tn supplementary budget, around USD 19bn, largely to replenish those reserves, alongside direct help with electricity and gas bills from July to September. Inflation gauges duly softened in Q2.

Chart 4 · Japan CPI inflation measures, year-on-yearGovernment subsidies kept the energy shock from diverting Japan's disinflationary trend. Sources: Bank of Japan, MIC, Haver Analytics. Data as of June 2026.

Subsidies cap near-term inflation and support real incomes, but they complicate the fiscal picture: the extra budget is funded entirely by deficit bonds, the proposed food-tax cut is unlikely before next April, and the government is promising to avoid extra issuance by relying on stronger revenues. That is a delicate act while the BOJ normalises and JGB yields are sensitive to debt worries.

The BOJ could argue there is no urgency, since real wage growth is still muted. But USDJPY rose above 163 at the end of July, a 40-year high, breaking the watched 160 to 162 range. The root cause is deeply negative real short-term rates in Japan against rising U.S. real rates, compounded by the fiscal overhang. Excess yen weakness raises imported inflation, erodes purchasing power and risks being read as policy vulnerability, inviting outflows. Intervention, deployed at scale in late July, smooths disorderly moves but rarely changes the trend without a credible policy mix. The authors therefore expect Japanese authorities to accelerate rate hikes to break the loop of outflows and a falling currency.

AustraliaWhat happens after front-loaded tightening

The call last November that the RBA could be an early and possibly the most hawkish G10 hiker in 2026 has played out, with three consecutive hikes in the first half as inflation stayed sticky. The challenge now is balancing still-elevated, largely oil-imported inflation against a softer growth backdrop, slowing consumption and a weakening housing market.

Sentiment that had picked up in 2025 reversed. The May 2026 Budget introduced two major property-investor tax changes: negative gearing limited mainly to new-build homes, and the 50% capital gains discount replaced by cost-base indexation plus a 30% minimum rate. The aim is to curb speculative demand for existing housing and redirect capital toward new supply; national house prices fell 0.4% month on month in June, the largest drop since 2022. Consumers also absorbed higher fuel costs, since Australia, though the world's second-largest gas exporter, is a price taker on oil.

Against this backdrop the RBA is expected to stay on hold in the second half. Its June minutes flagged three risks: oil feeding through to inflation, persistently weak productivity, and a potentially material housing downturn following the tax overhaul. With inflation tracking the bank's Q2 forecast, these point to an on-hold decision in coming months.

IndiaCan the structural growth story survive the AI era?

India remains one of Asia's strongest structural stories, but the macro narrative has become harder. Years of reforms, corporate deleveraging, favourable demographics and a thriving IT sector have left fundamentals robust, yet momentum is fading, and investors are asking harder questions about India's place in the AI era and a tougher geopolitical backdrop.

The AI debate has two strands. First, India's technology ecosystem is concentrated in IT services, software and business-process outsourcing, which face disruption as generative AI automates previously outsourced work. Second, India is under-represented in the AI hardware chain, so it is less positioned to capture the capex boom benefiting its neighbours.

Geopolitics did not help. In August 2025 the U.S. imposed a 25% tariff on Indian goods, later raised to 50% over Russian oil purchases. Negotiations succeeded in February 2026, cutting reciprocal tariffs to 18% and removing the Russia-linked penalties, but the Iran conflict then delivered a fresh shock to the world's second-largest crude importer, which buys roughly 85 to 90% of its oil abroad. The result was heavy portfolio outflows despite resilient fundamentals, and the rupee fell about 12% against the dollar over twelve months.

Near-term momentum should improve, helped by the U.S.–India trade agreement, continued infrastructure spending and fiscal consolidation. The RBI may also face less pressure to tighten than peers, offering modest support to domestic financial conditions.

ASEANSoutheast Asia: a region of divergence

ASEAN is no longer moving in lockstep. The divide runs between economies positioned to capture technology and capital inflows and those grappling with external financing pressure, weaker balance of payments or lost investor confidence.

Frontrunners: Singapore and Malaysia. Both are deeply integrated into the semiconductor and electronics chain and have delivered strong high-tech growth. Singapore's role as a financial hub keeps attracting capital, backed by fiscal buffers and institutional credibility. Malaysia benefits from sustained FDI into electronics and supply-chain relocation, and as a net energy exporter it was cushioned from the oil shock. These advantages are largely structural, so both should keep their lead.

Laggards: Indonesia, Thailand and the Philippines. Indonesia, long a favourite, saw a surprising collapse in investor confidence in 2026; outflows continue and the rupiah has yet to find a durable floor despite emergency rate hikes, and recovery of confidence may take considerable time given political uncertainty. Thailand faces a broad balance-of-payments squeeze from energy costs, softer tourism and thin foreign interest, with a sharply weaker current account and baht; its equity rally rested largely on a single AI-chain company. The Philippines is held back by energy vulnerability, weak investment, fiscal execution issues, infrastructure bottlenecks and a thin manufacturing base, with remittances the main support.

EquitiesExceptional earnings growth to outweigh near-term volatility

A positioning unwind across the AI infrastructure theme will keep Asian emerging-market equities volatile near term, but the fundamentals favour a positive stance on South Korea, Taiwan and China. Asia ex-Japan is in an AI infrastructure earnings super-cycle thanks to its grip on bottlenecks in logic, memory, networking and power, and compute remains in shortage. MSCI Asia ex-Japan earnings are expected to grow 50 to 55% in 2026 and 25 to 30% in 2027 at roughly 10.5x forward earnings; South Korea is expected to grow earnings 345 to 355% this year and 38 to 42% next, at just over 5x.

MSCI China has lagged this year but could do better in the second half. The macro recovery is underwhelming, yet the micro picture is improving: the food-delivery and fast-commerce price war is moderating faster than expected, stabilising earnings estimates, and agentic AI adoption is showing real progress. At 10.4x forward earnings, China is a useful counterweight to the chip-heavy Korean and Taiwanese markets.

Japan is a broadly neutral call with preferred pockets in banks, industrials and technology. A managed de-escalation in the Middle East should broaden performance into cyclicals and a moderately weaker yen helps earnings, but the bias toward BOJ hikes favours banks over the broad market, and TOPIX at 17x forward earnings looks rich against history.

CreditCarry remains king, with Asia high yield a preferred opportunity

Asia credit outperformed broader fixed income in the first half, helped by short duration in investment grade and attractive carry in high yield. Carry should stay the dominant return driver in a higher-for-longer rates world: over the next twelve months total returns of roughly 5.0 to 5.5% for Asia IG and 7 to 8% for Asia HY are expected, supported by all-in yields and much healthier fundamentals than during the recent default cycle.

Asia high yield is singled out as one of the region's most compelling opportunities. After the record default wave, first-half total returns approached 4% with defaults near zero; yields of about 7.8%, stronger balance sheets and a friendlier refinancing backdrop should keep defaults contained, and a roughly 35bp yield premium over CEMBI HY supports relative-value demand. Preferred themes include Hong Kong real estate, Macau gaming, Japanese life insurers, and India and Indonesia USD credit. AUD-denominated investment-grade bonds yielding around 5.5 to 6.0% also look attractive now that the RBA's tightening is largely done.

CurrenciesDiverging fundamentals, diverging currency paths

Asia FX is increasingly a story of divergence. Differences in external balances, AI exposure, policy credibility and capital flows are producing distinct currency paths.

Moderate CNH appreciation into 2027 is expected, gradual and managed: strong exports have widened the current-account surplus and exporters are more willing to convert dollar proceeds into yuan. On JPY the stance is neutral, with USDJPY expected to hover around 160 as authorities prioritise stability; that assumes three to four BOJ hikes over twelve months taking policy rates to about 2%, and failure to follow through would leave the yen vulnerable. AUD is viewed constructively over the medium term on terms of trade and fiscal discipline, even as its carry edge narrows.

Caution remains on INR, where outflow pressure persists and limited AI participation constrains appeal, even if depreciation slows with measures such as the RBI's FCNR deposit scheme. Most ASEAN currencies warrant caution given outflows and external vulnerabilities, with SGD the exception: a MAS tightening bias, continued inflows and the managed exchange-rate regime should keep it resilient.
